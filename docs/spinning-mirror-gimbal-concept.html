Spinning mirror gimbal

Concept only, nothing built. A round mirror inside a ring, the ring on a vertical shaft: two axes, both fast. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom.

Tilt drive: Mirror:

At low time scale you can follow the mechanism; push it to 1 and the wall pattern is what the eye would see (the screen here is capped at 60 fps, a real wall would blur even more). The wall is a dark screen at 520 mm, the Sun a fixed direction from the upper left; the beam only appears while the mirror faces the Sun.

What is being proposed

Outer frame

A 160 mm ring on a vertical shaft, turned by one motor underneath the table. It carries everything above it.

Inner mirror

A 130 mm disc on an axle through its diameter, fixed across the ring. The axle is horizontal and turns with the ring, so the mirror makes a second, independent rotation.

The catch

The frame spins, so the hinge axle spins around with it. Whatever drives the mirror has to ride along or reach through the shaft. That is the main design decision, shown as A and B above.

Option C: let the air drive it

What the view shows

The mirror is cut into four quarter plates, each shifted a few millimetres along its normal: an S on one side of the shaft and a reversed S on the other, tied by a thin spine along the axle. The blue arrows are the air as the frame sees it.

Why four, not two

The air flows opposite ways on the two sides of the shaft, because the ring moves one way there and the other way on the far side. Two offset halves would get opposite pushes that cancel. Mirror-image staggers on the two sides make the pushes add.

What the simulation does

Nothing here is computed aerodynamics. The tilt speed is set by the ratio slider and slowly wanders, to show a loose coupling that never repeats exactly. Whether flat staggered plates make any useful torque is a bench test: print one and spin it on a drill. A curved Savonius profile would do better than flat.

Option D: real randomness

The idea

Nothing drives the mirror. It hangs on a free hinge, an optional small weight on its edge, and the single motor's speed jitters at random. At steady speed the mirror just settles flat. With the speed wobbling it gets pumped, swings, then tumbles over the top in random bursts and reverses.

Is it chaos or noise?

Both help. The mirror's own equation is nonlinear, so small differences grow, and the motor's jitter adds true randomness. Lower friction and more jitter give more tumbling. Below about 20 to 30 percent jitter it mostly swings; at 45 and up it tumbles in runs of several turns either way.

How to make the jitter

Drive the motor from a microcontroller that sets its speed from a slow random walk, or just feed it a noisy supply. No sensors or extra motors. The physics is the equation for a hinged disc in a spinning frame, with the weight and friction you set. Real bearings add their own stick and slip.

Things that matter once it is fast

Motors

Steppers are the wrong tool here: a NEMA 17 loses torque above a few hundred rpm and the 28BYJ is about 15 rpm. A brushed DC or a brushless motor with an ESC reaches thousands of rpm cheaply. Speed control is then by PWM, with a Hall or optical sensor if you want the speed read back.

Balance

A round mirror on a diametral axle is balanced about the hinge by symmetry, so the thing to get right is the ring and its mounts. Imbalance force grows with speed squared. Design the ring symmetrical, add a counterweight for any motor, and trim with small screws.

Loads

Ring tip at 3000 rpm and 80 mm radius is about 25 m/s. A 130 mm, 3 mm acrylic mirror weighs about 47 g and has about 5e-5 kg m² about its hinge. With both axes near 300 rpm the gyroscopic torque on the hinge is about 0.05 N m, small, but it all lands on two bearings.

Mirror material

Use acrylic mirror, not glass. A glass disc that lets go at speed is dangerous. Back-to-back acrylic mirrors give two silvered faces, which makes the beam appear twice per turn.

What you see

A mirror turning by an angle sweeps the beam by twice that. A simple ratio such as 8:5 draws a closed rose on the wall, an irrational one slowly fills a band. Fast enough, persistence of vision turns it into a glowing patch that breathes.

Safety

Guard the ring (a clear cylinder works), keep fingers away from the gap at the shaft, and remember a reflected Sun beam sweeping across a room can dazzle. Fast sweeps help; a fixed one would not.

Questions to settle before any CAD

  • Target speeds: gentle (tens of rpm) or really fast (hundreds to thousands)? This picks motors and bearings.
  • Independent speeds (A or C) or one locked pattern (B)?
  • Is this stand-alone, or does the whole head go on the Sun-tracking base later?
  • Ring size: 160 mm here, with a 130 mm mirror. Print on the P1S, or turn it from aluminium?
  • Slip ring acceptable (A), or must it run without any wiring up the shaft (B, C)?
